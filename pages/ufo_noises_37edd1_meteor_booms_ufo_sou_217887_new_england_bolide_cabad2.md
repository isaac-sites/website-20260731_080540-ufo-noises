## Introduction

The New England bolide of 30 May 2026 became a textbook example of how investigators distinguish an atmospheric explosion from an earthquake. Across Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and neighbouring states, residents reported a powerful boom, rattling windows and brief shaking. Many initially suspected an earthquake because the physical effects resembled a shallow seismic event. ## Why residents suspected an earthquake

For many people, the event was experienced as sound and vibration rather than as a visible fireball. Reports described one or two loud explosive booms, houses shaking, windows vibrating and pets reacting suddenly. Because the blast was heard across several states, many witnesses assumed an earthquake or industrial explosion had occurred.

This reaction was understandable for several reasons:

* Many people never saw the meteor because it passed in daylight or outside their field of view.
* The sonic boom reached observers after the meteor had already disappeared.
* Ground vibration from the pressure wave felt similar to a minor earthquake.

The event illustrates why loud [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) booms often enter UFO or mystery-noise discussions before scientific evidence becomes available.

## How atmospheric and seismic signals differ

The decisive evidence came from comparing how earthquakes and atmospheric shock waves appear on monitoring networks.

An earthquake produces seismic waves travelling through rock at several kilometres per second. These waves create characteristic patterns on seismographs that allow scientists to locate the underground rupture and estimate its magnitude.

A bolide explosion behaves differently. The meteor generates a pressure wave travelling through the atmosphere at roughly the speed of sound. When this wave reaches the ground it can shake buildings and occasionally register on seismic instruments, but the signal differs markedly from that of an earthquake.

According to the U.S. Geological Survey, investigators look for several distinguishing features:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* Either no earthquake signal or only a short, high-frequency disturbance on seismic records.
* Timing between stations that matches the slower speed of sound through air rather than seismic-wave propagation through rock.
* Widespread reports of shaking without any corresponding earthquake located by seismic networks.
* Frequent reports of loud bangs or explosive noises, suggesting an atmospheric rather than geological source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/sonic-booms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Geological Survey Sonic Booms &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological Survey Sonic Booms &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span></span></span>

</div>

In the New England case, the combination of widespread felt reports and the absence of a matching earthquake immediately pointed investigators away from a tectonic explanation.

## What confirmed the bolide explanation

The atmospheric explanation became increasingly convincing as independent lines of evidence converged.

### Eyewitness reports matched a meteor

Hundreds of observers reported an exceptionally bright daytime fireball crossing the sky before the boom. ### NASA estimated the explosion energy

NASA reported that the meteor entered the atmosphere at roughly 75,000 mph (about 121,000 km/h), fragmented at an altitude of around 40 miles (64 km), and released energy equivalent to approximately 300 tons of TNT. ### Seismic data ruled out an earthquake

Perhaps the strongest evidence came from what was missing. Although many residents felt shaking, the regional seismic network did not record an earthquake consistent with the reports. ## Why the investigation mattered

The New England bolide demonstrated that no single observation is enough to identify the source of an unusual boom. Witness testimony alone suggested an earthquake to some people and an unidentified aerial phenomenon to others. Seismic records alone showed only that no conventional earthquake had occurred.

Only by combining several independent datasets could investigators reach a reliable conclusion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* seismic monitoring showing no earthquake;
* eyewitness reports describing a bright fireball;
* satellite detection of the atmospheric flash;
* astronomical analysis of the meteor's trajectory and fragmentation;
* physical modelling showing that the estimated explosion energy could account for the widespread sonic boom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/sonic-booms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[usgs.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Geological Survey Sonic Booms &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological Survey Sonic Booms &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span></span></span>

</div>

For investigations into mysterious aerial sounds, this multi-sensor approach is now standard practice. The New England event provides a clear example of how an apparently earthquake-like disturbance can instead be traced to a natural bolide exploding high in the atmosphere, resolving initial speculation without invoking unknown or extraordinary causes.

      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
