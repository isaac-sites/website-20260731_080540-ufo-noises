## Introduction

Many reports of **The Hum** are described as an external [low-frequency]({{ 'low-frequency/' | relative_url }}) sound, yet medical research suggests that a significant proportion may instead originate within the listener's own auditory system. In these cases, the experience resembles a rare form of **low-frequency subjective tinnitus**—the [perception]({{ 'perception/' | relative_url }}) of sound without a corresponding external acoustic source. This possibility is especially relevant when the sound is heard by only one person, changes with head movement, interacts with other sounds, or cannot be detected by sensitive acoustic measurements. At the same time, not every Hum report fits this pattern. Well-documented cases such as Windsor, Ontario demonstrate that genuine environmental low-frequency noise can also occur. The evidence therefore points towards multiple mechanisms rather than a single explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0326818" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the potential sources of a low-frequency sound percept that only a few can perceive &#124; PLOS OneMarch 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

One of the strongest arguments for an internal origin comes from patterns that would be difficult to explain if the sound were produced entirely by an external source.

Researchers studying people who identify as "hearers" found that many describe distinctive behaviours, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the Hum disappearing or changing after turning the head;
* the perceived tone forming beats or interference patterns with external sounds;
* delays before the sound returns after air travel or changes in pressure;
* hearing the Hum when nearby people cannot, even in otherwise quiet environments.

</div>

These observations are consistent with changes occurring within the ear or auditory pathways rather than with a fixed environmental sound. In a questionnaire study involving more than 160 people reporting the Hum, around three-quarters displayed at least one of these characteristic features, leading the author to argue that many cases represent an unusual form of tinnitus rather than an unidentified external signal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27488996/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manifestations of a low-frequency sound of unknown origin perceived worldwide, also known as &quot;the Hum&quot; or the &quot;Taos Hum&quot; - PubMedJu...</span></span></span>

Head movement is particularly interesting because altering the position of the neck, jaw or inner ear can temporarily change sensory input reaching the brain. Many forms of tinnitus are known to vary with movements of the jaw or head, reflecting interactions between hearing pathways and nerves controlling muscles and balance rather than changes in the outside environment. Although this does not prove tinnitus in every Hum case, it provides a biologically plausible explanation for experiences that seem to move or disappear with body position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0326818" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the potential sources of a low-frequency sound percept that only a few can perceive &#124; PLOS OneMarch 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

## How Low-Frequency Tinnitus Differs From Ordinary Noise

Most people associate tinnitus with high-pitched ringing, but tinnitus can also occur as a deep humming, rumbling or droning sound. Low-frequency tinnitus is less common and can therefore be mistaken for distant machinery, electrical equipment or even aircraft.

Several features distinguish it from ordinary environmental noise:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **It often lacks a clear direction.** People may be unable to point towards where the sound is coming from.
* **Only one person may hear it.** Others in the same room may hear nothing at all.
* **The loudness may fluctuate without changes in the surroundings.**
* **Quiet indoor environments often make it more noticeable**, not because the sound becomes stronger but because there is less competing background noise.

</div>

Unlike genuine low-frequency environmental noise, subjective tinnitus cannot usually be recorded with microphones because the sound exists as a perception generated somewhere along the auditory system. This distinction explains why extensive acoustic surveys sometimes fail to detect anything matching a sufferer's experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/026309238300200203" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsA Classification of Environmental “Hums” and Low Frequency Tinnitus - R.E. ## What Hearing Tests Can and Cannot Show

Medical assessment aims to determine whether the sound is more likely to be internal or external rather than assuming either explanation from the outset.

Audiologists may use:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* conventional and low-frequency hearing tests;
* tinnitus pitch-matching, where patients compare their perceived sound with generated tones;
* examination for hearing loss, ear disease or middle-ear disorders;
* assessment of whether jaw movement, neck movement or pressure changes alter the sound.

</div>

Earlier work comparing people reporting environmental Hums with patients already diagnosed with low-frequency tinnitus found substantial overlap in the frequencies they perceived. While this did not prove that all Hum reports were tinnitus, it demonstrated that many complaints closely resembled recognised clinical cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/026309238300200203" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsA Classification of Environmental “Hums” and Low Frequency Tinnitus - R.E. Walford, 1983...</span></span></span>

Recent research has strengthened this view. A 2026 study examined people reporting persistent low-frequency sound percepts, measuring unusually sensitive hearing and spontaneous otoacoustic emissions—tiny sounds naturally generated by the inner ear. The researchers found little evidence that exceptional low-frequency hearing or self-generated ear emissions explained most cases. Instead, they concluded that **subjective low-frequency tinnitus is often the most likely explanation when no external sound source can be identified**, while emphasising that genuine external sounds remain possible in some situations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0326818" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the potential sources of a low-frequency sound percept that only a few can perceive &#124; PLOS OneMarch 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why One Explanation Cannot Fit Every Hum Report

The tinnitus hypothesis explains many personal Hum experiences, but it cannot account for every well-investigated case.

Some communities have documented measurable low-frequency sound or vibration affecting multiple residents simultaneously. In those situations, investigators have identified industrial equipment, ventilation systems or other infrastructure as likely contributors. Such findings demonstrate that authentic environmental Hums exist alongside medically generated auditory perceptions.

Conversely, many reports share features far more typical of tinnitus:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* only a minority of residents perceive the sound;
* the sound changes with head or jaw movement;
* extensive environmental measurements fail to detect a matching acoustic signal;
* clinical examination finds patterns compatible with auditory dysfunction.

</div>

## What This Means for UFO Speculation

Within discussions of [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) aerial phenomena, the Hum has sometimes been presented as evidence of hidden aircraft, experimental propulsion systems or extraterrestrial technology. The existence of low-frequency tinnitus offers a more ordinary explanation for many reports that appear mysterious at first.

Importantly, recognising an internal auditory mechanism does not imply that every Hum complaint is imagined or psychological. Subjective tinnitus is a genuine neurological perception that can be persistent, intrusive and distressing. At the same time, documented environmental Hum cases show that external low-frequency sound also occurs in the real world.

The most evidence-based approach is therefore to investigate each report individually. If acoustic measurements repeatedly fail to identify an external source and the perceived sound varies with head movement, hearing tests or other recognised tinnitus characteristics, an internal auditory origin becomes increasingly plausible. Where measurable low-frequency sound is present and affects multiple listeners, environmental explanations deserve equal attention. Current research supports this case-by-case view far more strongly than any single theory linking all Hum reports to UFOs or other extraordinary causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0326818" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[plos.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the potential sources of a low-frequency sound percept that only a few can perceive &#124; PLOS OneMarch 27, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 27, 2026</span></span></span>
