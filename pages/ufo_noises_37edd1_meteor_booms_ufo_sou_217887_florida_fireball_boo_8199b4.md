## Introduction

The northern Florida fireball of 21 February 2015 is a useful case study in how an event that initially sounds mysterious can be reconstructed using hundreds of public observations. For many people, the defining experience was not the brilliant meteor itself but the delayed boom that rattled windows and shook homes. Those reports briefly prompted speculation about explosions or other unusual aerial activity, yet the pattern of eyewitness accounts ultimately helped investigators identify where the meteor ended its flight. Rather than being random anecdotes, the combined reports formed a geographical map that closely matched the fireball's trajectory and the area where its strongest sonic boom reached the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/2015/02/florida-fireball-with-boom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Florida Fireball with Boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFlorida Fireball with Boom - American Meteor SocietyFebruary 22, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2015</span></span></span>

## What witnesses saw and heard

Shortly before 11 p.m. Eastern Time on 21 February 2015, residents across northern Florida and southern Georgia observed an exceptionally bright fireball crossing the night sky. Witnesses described a brilliant white or greenish streak, with some reporting that the landscape briefly lit up as though by lightning. The meteor remained visible for only a few seconds before disappearing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/2015/02/florida-fireball-with-boom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Florida Fireball with Boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFlorida Fireball with Boom - American Meteor SocietyFebruary 22, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2015</span></span></span>

The sound came later. Dozens of people reported hearing a powerful boom between several seconds and a few minutes after the flash, depending on their distance from the meteor's terminal path. Typical descriptions included:

* A cannon-like blast.
* A distant explosion.
* Windows rattling.
* Houses briefly shaking.
* Multiple booms rather than a single report.

</div>

The American Meteor Society (AMS) collected more than 230 eyewitness reports, with over 48 specifically describing delayed sonic effects. Individual accounts from Starke, Sanderson and Lake City consistently mentioned shaking buildings and loud explosive noises rather than continuous aircraft sounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/2015/02/florida-fireball-with-boom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Florida Fireball with Boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFlorida Fireball with Boom - American Meteor SocietyFebruary 22, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2015</span></span></span>

## Where the strongest boom reports clustered

The most revealing feature of the event was not simply the number of reports but their geographical distribution.

Using the submitted observations, the AMS produced separate maps showing visual sightings and delayed sonic-boom reports. While people across a broad region saw the meteor, reports of the strongest pressure wave were concentrated much more tightly near the end of the meteor's atmospheric flight. Many of the loudest house-shaking reports came from the Lake City area, with additional reports extending towards western Jacksonville.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/2015/02/florida-fireball-with-boom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Florida Fireball with Boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFlorida Fireball with Boom - American Meteor SocietyFebruary 22, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2015</span></span></span>

This clustering reflected a well-known property of meteor-generated shock waves. The loudest booms generally originate near the final stages of flight, where the meteoroid has descended to lower altitude and often fragments. Instead of being spread evenly beneath the entire visible path, the strongest pressure waves tend to reach the ground in a more focused region.

In Florida, that meant the pattern of delayed boom reports effectively highlighted the meteor's terminal section even before detailed instrumental analysis was completed. The witness distribution therefore became evidence rather than merely anecdote.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/2015/02/florida-fireball-with-boom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Florida Fireball with Boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFlorida Fireball with Boom - American Meteor SocietyFebruary 22, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2015</span></span></span>

## How investigators reconstructed the flight

The investigation combined two complementary sources of information.

First, hundreds of eyewitness reports allowed the AMS to estimate the meteor's apparent track across the sky. Those reports indicated that the object travelled from the south-west towards the north-east, ending roughly 30 miles (about 48 kilometres) west of Jacksonville.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/2015/02/florida-fireball-with-boom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Florida Fireball with Boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFlorida Fireball with Boom - American Meteor SocietyFebruary 22, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2015</span></span></span>

Second, NASA's Sky Sentinel all-sky camera network independently recorded the event from two observing stations in Melbourne, Florida. The camera-based triangulation broadly agreed with the witness-derived trajectory, providing confidence that the public reports had accurately captured the meteor's path. NASA estimated the object was roughly one foot (around 30 centimetres) across, weighed about 100 pounds (45 kilograms), travelled at approximately 40,000 miles per hour (64,000 km/h), and burned up before reaching the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/watch-the-skies/2015/02/23/fireball-over-jacksonville-florida-on-feb-21/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fireball over Jacksonville, Florida on Feb. 21</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fireball over Jacksonville, Florida on Feb. 21 - NASA...</span></span></span>

## Why this case matters for reports of mysterious aerial booms

The Florida event illustrates why meteor-generated sonic booms are sometimes mistaken for [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) aerial phenomena.

Many residents never saw the meteor itself. They experienced only a sudden explosion-like noise, vibrating windows or a brief shaking of their homes. Without the earlier visual cue, those sounds could easily be interpreted as industrial explosions, [military aircraft]({{ 'military-flights/' | relative_url }}) or something more mysterious.

What distinguished this case was the density of reporting. Instead of isolated accounts, investigators received hundreds of independent observations that could be compared geographically. Visual reports defined the meteor's path, while delayed boom reports identified where the strongest shock wave reached the surface. Together they produced a coherent reconstruction that matched instrumental recordings rather than leaving the event unexplained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/2015/02/florida-fireball-with-boom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Florida Fireball with Boom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFlorida Fireball with Boom - American Meteor SocietyFebruary 22, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2015</span></span></span>

The Florida fireball therefore remains an instructive example of how organised citizen reporting can transform what initially appears to be an unexplained loud aerial event into a well-documented meteor case.
