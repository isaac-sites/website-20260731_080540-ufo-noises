## Introduction

A steady, low electrical hum is one of the most common sounds described by people who report a nearby unidentified flying object (UFO). Yet that same description closely matches the sound produced by electrical transformers and substations. This overlap is significant because transformer hum is a well-understood physical phenomenon that can travel surprisingly long distances, penetrate buildings and become difficult to locate accurately. In some situations, a listener may genuinely perceive the sound as coming from above or from an apparently empty area, even when its true source is fixed infrastructure rather than anything airborne. This does not explain every UFO report, but it does provide a plausible, evidence-based mechanism for a subset of cases in which the principal auditory feature is a continuous [low-frequency]({{ 'low-frequency/' | relative_url }}) hum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-policy-statement-for-electricity-networks-infrastructure-en-5-2025/national-policy-statement-for-electricity-networks-infrastructure-en-5-2025-accessible-webpage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Policy Statement for electricity networks infrastructure (EN-5), 2025 (accessible webpage) - GOV.UKJanuary 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2026</span></span></span>

## How transformer vibration sounds

Electrical transformers generate sound because alternating current causes their laminated steel cores to expand and contract by microscopic amounts through a process called **magnetostriction**. Although the movement is tiny, it occurs repeatedly with every cycle of the electrical supply, creating a stable tonal vibration that listeners recognise as transformer hum. In countries using a 50 Hz mains supply, the dominant audible tone commonly appears around 100 Hz because the mechanical deformation occurs twice during each electrical cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.samaterials.com/content/magnetostriction-and-transformers.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: samaterials.com">[samaterials.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">samaterials.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stanford Advanced Materials Magnetostriction and Transformers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stanford Advanced Materials Magnetostriction and Transformers</span></span></span>

* A deep, steady drone rather than a roar.
* More noticeable than directional.
* Constant in pitch with little fluctuation.

These characteristics resemble many UFO witness descriptions that compare the sound to an electrical installation, generator or distant industrial machine rather than an aircraft engine. The similarity is notable because witnesses frequently choose the same real-world comparison: "like a transformer". The comparison itself does not identify the source—it simply reflects how closely the acoustic signatures resemble one another.

Government guidance on electricity infrastructure also recognises transformer hum as a normal by-product of substations and notes that whether it is audible outside depends on transformer design, installation and surrounding sound attenuation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-policy-statement-for-electricity-networks-infrastructure-en-5-2025/national-policy-statement-for-electricity-networks-infrastructure-en-5-2025-accessible-webpage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Policy Statement for electricity networks infrastructure (EN-5), 2025 (accessible webpage) - GOV.UKJanuary 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why the source can seem overhead

The most confusing aspect of transformer hum is often not the sound itself but its apparent location.

Human hearing determines direction by comparing tiny differences in arrival time and loudness between the two ears. Those cues become much weaker at low frequencies because the wavelengths are very long. As a result, listeners often struggle to determine whether the sound is coming from in front, behind, above or below.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.environmental-protection.org.uk/policy-areas/noise/environmental-noise/low-frequency-noise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: environmental-protection.org.uk">[Environmental Protection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">environmental-protection.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Environmental Protection Low Frequency Noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Environmental ProtectionLow Frequency Noise - Environmental Protection UK...</span></span></span>

Several environmental effects can make the problem worse:

* **Long-distance propagation.** Low-frequency sound loses less energy over distance than many higher-frequency sounds, allowing it to remain audible after sharper components have disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.environmental-protection.org.uk/policy-areas/noise/environmental-noise/low-frequency-noise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: environmental-protection.org.uk">[Environmental Protection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">environmental-protection.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Environmental Protection Low Frequency Noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Environmental ProtectionLow Frequency Noise - Environmental Protection UK...</span></span></span>
* **Building resonance.** Walls, floors and rooms may amplify particular frequencies, making the hum stronger indoors than outside or in one room but not another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.environmental-protection.org.uk/policy-areas/noise/environmental-noise/low-frequency-noise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: environmental-protection.org.uk">[Environmental Protection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">environmental-protection.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Environmental Protection Low Frequency Noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Environmental ProtectionLow Frequency Noise - Environmental Protection UK...</span></span></span>
* **Reflections.** Buildings, terrain and other structures can reflect low-frequency sound, making it appear detached from its physical origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.environmental-protection.org.uk/policy-areas/noise/environmental-noise/low-frequency-noise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: environmental-protection.org.uk">[Environmental Protection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">environmental-protection.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Environmental Protection Low Frequency Noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Environmental ProtectionLow Frequency Noise - Environmental Protection UK...</span></span></span>
* **Structural vibration.** Instead of hearing only airborne sound, people may perceive vibration transmitted through the ground or building framework, making localisation even less reliable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.environmental-protection.org.uk/policy-areas/noise/environmental-noise/low-frequency-noise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: environmental-protection.org.uk">[Environmental Protection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">environmental-protection.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Environmental Protection Low Frequency Noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Environmental ProtectionLow Frequency Noise - Environmental Protection UK...</span></span></span>

If a witness is already focused on an unusual light or object in the sky, the brain may naturally associate an otherwise ambiguous hum with that visual stimulus, even if the sound originates from nearby electrical infrastructure.

## Why transformer hum is often mistaken for something unusual

Transformer hum has several properties that encourage misidentification without requiring any unusual acoustic phenomenon.

First, it is generally continuous rather than intermittent. Aircraft engines usually change pitch as the aircraft moves relative to the observer, whereas transformer hum tends to remain remarkably stable.

Second, the source is stationary while the listener may move. As a person walks around, [room resonance]({{ 'room-resonance/' | relative_url }}) and reflections can make the apparent direction shift unexpectedly. This can create the impression that the sound is following them or has no identifiable origin. Environmental health organisations note that tracing persistent low-frequency hums is often difficult even for investigators using conventional sound measurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.environmental-protection.org.uk/policy-areas/noise/environmental-noise/low-frequency-noise/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: environmental-protection.org.uk">[environmental-protection.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">environmental-protection.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Environmental Protection Low Frequency Noise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Environmental ProtectionLow Frequency Noise - Environmental Protection UK...</span></span></span>

Finally, transformer hum often becomes most noticeable at night. ## Clues that distinguish infrastructure noise

Although transformer hum can resemble [reported UFO sounds]({{ 'sound-reports/' | relative_url }}), several practical clues favour an electrical source over an airborne one.

**Features consistent with transformer or substation noise include:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The hum maintains a nearly constant pitch.
* The sound continues after any visual sighting has ended.
* It is strongest near buildings, substations or overhead electrical infrastructure.
* Different observers disagree about the direction it comes from.
* The hum becomes louder in certain rooms or locations indoors.

</div>

**Features that would be less consistent with transformer hum include:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A sound that accelerates or changes pitch rapidly in synchrony with an apparently moving object.
* A clearly directional source that tracks a visible object's motion across the sky.
* Brief, highly localised bursts rather than a persistent drone.
* Complex tonal changes unlike the stable spectrum expected from transformer vibration.

</div>

None of these features alone proves or disproves a UFO interpretation, but they help distinguish a fixed environmental noise source from an object genuinely producing sound while moving through the air.

## What acoustics research suggests

Research into environmental low-frequency noise shows that persistent hums can be surprisingly difficult to identify without specialised measurements. Standard sound-level readings may underestimate annoyance because low-frequency energy behaves differently from higher-frequency noise, and identifying the exact source may require narrow-band analysis or microphone arrays designed specifically for localisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/026309238900800201" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[sagepub.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsAnnoyance Due to Environmental Low Frequency Noise and Source Location — A Case Study - R.N. Recent engineering work has focused on advanced microphone-array techniques capable of mapping transformer sound fields in three dimensions, illustrating that even known infrastructure sources require specialised tools to pinpoint accurately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003682X24005024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Locating and reconstructing transformer low-frequency noises with a 3D, six-microphone array - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Within the context of UFO reports, this evidence supports a cautious interpretation. A witness who accurately reports hearing a deep, steady electrical hum may indeed be describing a real sound. However, the presence of that sound alone does not indicate an unidentified craft because ordinary transformer vibration can produce remarkably similar acoustic characteristics while simultaneously making its own location difficult to determine.
