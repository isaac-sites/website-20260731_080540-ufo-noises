## Introduction

A witness who is absolutely certain that a UFO emitted a humming, buzzing or mechanical sound may be giving a sincere account, but confidence alone does not establish that every remembered detail is accurate. Decades of psychological research show that confidence and memory accuracy are related in complex ways. Under some carefully controlled conditions, an immediate, uncontaminated memory can be both highly confident and highly accurate. However, confidence can also increase after repeated retelling, discussion, media exposure or suggestive questioning even when specific details have drifted from the original experience. This distinction is especially important when evaluating reports of unusual sounds associated with unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP), where the sound source, timing and acoustic characteristics are often difficult to verify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100616686966/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychologicalscience.org">[Association for Psychological Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychologicalscience.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association for Psychological ScienceThe Relationship Between Eyewitness Confidence and Identification Accuracy: A New Synthesis - Psycho...</span></span></span>

## How confidence grows through repeated recall

Memory is not a fixed recording that is replayed unchanged. Each act of remembering involves reconstruction, and the recalled version can become the basis for later memories. As a result, people often become more fluent and more certain when describing an event repeatedly, even if some details have gradually changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.1263" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryRepeated recall, retention interval and the accuracy–confidence relation in eyewitness memory - Odinot - 2006 - Appli...</span></span></span>

Research on eyewitness memory has found that repetition itself can increase confidence in misinformation. Repeated exposure to the same claim makes it feel more familiar and believable, even when the claim originated after the original event rather than during it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000169181100223X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Repetition, not number of sources, increases both susceptibility to misinformation and confidence in the accuracy of eyewitn...</span></span></span>

This does not imply deliberate exaggeration. Instead, confidence often reflects how coherent and well-rehearsed a memory has become rather than whether every remembered sound matches what was originally heard.

## Why confidence and accuracy can diverge

The relationship between confidence and accuracy is more nuanced than the simple assumption that "confident means correct" or "confident means unreliable." Psychological research shows both positions are too simplistic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100616686966/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychologicalscience.org">[Association for Psychological Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychologicalscience.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association for Psychological ScienceThe Relationship Between Eyewitness Confidence and Identification Accuracy: A New Synthesis - Psycho...</span></span></span>

Several mechanisms can produce a gap between certainty and sound accuracy.

### Source confusion

People may accurately remember hearing a sound while becoming less certain where that sound originated. A distant aircraft engine, industrial machinery, insects or wind-induced vibration may later become mentally linked with the unusual object that was being observed.

The memory of the sound itself may remain genuine, while the remembered source gradually shifts through repeated reconstruction. This phenomenon, often discussed as source monitoring or source confusion, is well established in memory research and becomes more likely when events are unexpected or ambiguous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK621592/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIReference Guide on Eyewitness Identification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference Manual on Scientific Evidence - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

### Confidence inflation after feedback

Confidence can increase when witnesses receive explicit or implicit confirmation from others.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* investigators appearing to endorse a description;
* friends reporting similar experiences;
* reading published UFO accounts that resemble one's own;
* online discussions reinforcing a particular interpretation.

</div>

Eyewitness research shows that confirming feedback can substantially inflate confidence without improving underlying memory accuracy. This is one reason forensic psychologists recommend recording a witness's initial confidence before outside influences accumulate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/topics/forensics-law-public-safety/eyewitness-accuracy-police-lineups" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Psychological Association Eyewitness accuracy in police lineups</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Psychological AssociationEyewitness accuracy in police lineupsApril 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 2014</span></span></span>

### Narrative coherence

Humans naturally prefer a coherent story over disconnected observations.

Instead of remembering:

> "I saw an unusual light, and separately I heard an unfamiliar noise."

people often come to remember:

> "The object made a loud electrical hum."

The second version is easier to understand, easier to tell and therefore easier to remember confidently, even if the original evidence linking the sound to the object was uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK621592/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIReference Guide on Eyewitness Identification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reference Manual on Scientific Evidence - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

## Evidence from eyewitness research

Most scientific evidence on confidence and memory comes from eyewitness studies rather than UFO investigations specifically. Although identifying a suspect differs from recalling an [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) aerial event, the underlying cognitive processes are highly relevant.

Early research consistently found that confidence alone was a poor predictor of whether individual memories were accurate. In some experiments, the statistical relationship between confidence and correctness was surprisingly weak, demonstrating that people could express high certainty about incorrect recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2708267/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eyewitness accuracy and confidence: within- versus between-subjects correlations - PubMed...</span></span></span>

More recent work has refined that conclusion rather than overturning it. Studies indicate that an immediate confidence judgement made under carefully controlled conditions—before feedback, repeated interviews or contamination—can provide useful information about accuracy. Once memories have been repeatedly discussed or influenced by later information, however, confidence becomes much less informative because it may have been inflated independently of memory accuracy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100616686966/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychologicalscience.org">[Association for Psychological Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychologicalscience.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association for Psychological ScienceThe Relationship Between Eyewitness Confidence and Identification Accuracy: A New Synthesis - Psycho...</span></span></span>

For historical UFO cases, witnesses are frequently interviewed days, months or even decades after the reported event. By then, opportunities for memory reconstruction are far greater than in the controlled conditions where confidence has stronger evidential value.

## What investigators should ask beyond certainty

Because confidence and accuracy are not identical, investigators examining [reported UFO sounds]({{ 'sound-reports/' | relative_url }}) should treat certainty as one piece of evidence rather than as proof.

More informative questions include:

* **When was the sound first noticed?** Determining whether the sound preceded, coincided with or followed the visual observation helps separate [perception]({{ 'perception/' | relative_url }}) from later reconstruction.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **How was the sound described immediately after the event?** Contemporary notes or recordings are generally more informative than memories recalled years later.
* **Could the witness identify the sound's direction?** Humans often struggle to localise low-frequency or distant sounds accurately.
* **Has the description changed over time?** Comparing early and later accounts can reveal whether details became more elaborate through repeated recall.
* **Were there possible conventional sound sources?** Nearby aircraft, traffic, industrial equipment, wildlife or weather conditions should be considered before attributing a sound to the observed object.

</div>

These questions do not assume the witness is mistaken. Instead, they recognise that sincerity and memory precision are separate issues.

## Confidence is evidence of belief, not proof of acoustic reality

A highly confident witness should not be dismissed simply because memory can change. Confidence provides valuable evidence that the person genuinely believes their account and may indicate that the experience was emotionally significant. What it cannot do, by itself, is verify that every remembered acoustic detail—including the timing, source, loudness or character of an alleged UFO noise—accurately reflects the original event.

For reports of unexplained aerial sounds, the strongest assessments combine witness testimony with contemporaneous notes, multiple independent observations, environmental conditions, audio recordings and other corroborating evidence whenever available. Confidence contributes to that assessment, but it cannot substitute for independent verification of what was actually heard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100616686966/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychologicalscience.org">[psychologicalscience.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychologicalscience.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association for Psychological ScienceThe Relationship Between Eyewitness Confidence and Identification Accuracy: A New Synthesis - Psycho...</span></span></span>
