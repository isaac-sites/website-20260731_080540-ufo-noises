## Introduction

Most meteor sounds are heard **minutes after** a brilliant fireball, when the shock wave finally reaches the ground. Yet a much rarer class of reports describes something very different: a faint hiss, crackle, sizzle or pop heard **at exactly the same moment** as the meteor flashes across the sky. These so-called **electrophonic meteor sounds** have intrigued astronomers for centuries because ordinary sound cannot travel from a meteor tens of kilometres overhead quickly enough to explain them. Rather than overturning physics, the reports have prompted researchers to investigate whether a meteor might generate electromagnetic energy that causes nearby objects—or even hair, glasses or foliage—to vibrate locally. Although the phenomenon has not been conclusively verified under controlled conditions, it remains one of the most unusual unresolved questions associated with bright meteors and is occasionally mistaken for a mysterious "UFO noise".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[amsmeteors.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

## Why ordinary sound cannot arrive with the flash

The central mystery begins with a simple physical constraint. Light from a meteor reaches an observer almost instantly, while sound in air travels at roughly 343 metres per second. A meteor burning 80–100 km above Earth cannot produce an audible hiss or crackle that arrives simultaneously through normal acoustic propagation.

This distinction is important because it separates two very different phenomena:

* **Delayed sonic booms** are well understood. They result from shock waves produced by large fireballs and typically arrive between about 1.5 and 4 minutes after the meteor is seen.
* **Instantaneous reports** describe sounds perceived during the visible flight itself, with no noticeable delay. These are the observations referred to as electrophonic sounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

Because the timing appears impossible for ordinary sound, early investigators often dismissed such reports as mistakes or coincidence. However, the remarkable consistency of many independent descriptions—often involving words such as "hissing", "sizzling", "swishing" or "crackling"—kept the question alive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930009995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[NASA Technical Reports Server]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Technical Reports Server Meteor fireball sounds identified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Technical Reports ServerMeteor fireball sounds identified - NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS)...</span></span></span>

## The electromagnetic vibration hypothesis

The leading explanation does not suggest that the meteor's sound itself travels instantaneously. Instead, it proposes that the meteor emits **very [low frequency]({{ 'low-frequency/' | relative_url }}) (VLF)** or **extremely low frequency (ELF)** electromagnetic waves.

Unlike sound, electromagnetic waves travel at essentially the speed of light. If sufficiently strong, they could reach an observer almost simultaneously with the visible meteor. The audible sound would then be created **locally**, not in the sky.

Researchers have proposed that the electromagnetic pulse could induce tiny vibrations in nearby objects acting as natural transducers, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* thin metal objects such as wire fences or spectacles;
* dry leaves, pine needles or grass;
* aluminium foil or lightweight conductors;
* even strands of human hair under favourable conditions.

</div>

The listener would therefore hear a nearby object vibrating, even though the trigger originated with the distant meteor. This neatly resolves the apparent conflict with the speed of sound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930009995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Technical Reports Server Meteor fireball sounds identified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Technical Reports ServerMeteor fireball sounds identified - NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS)...</span></span></span>

One influential series of studies by physicist Colin Keay argued that turbulent plasma surrounding exceptionally bright meteors could distort Earth's magnetic field and generate VLF radiation. Laboratory experiments also demonstrated that ordinary objects can convert electrical excitation into audible vibrations, making the basic transduction mechanism physically plausible even if the atmospheric source remains debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930009995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[NASA Technical Reports Server]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Technical Reports Server Meteor fireball sounds identified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Technical Reports ServerMeteor fireball sounds identified - NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS)...</span></span></span>

## Why witnesses often describe hissing rather than explosions

Reported electrophonic sounds differ noticeably from delayed meteor booms.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/srep41251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Photoacoustic Sounds from Meteors &#124; Scientific Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Photoacoustic Sounds from Meteors &#124; Scientific Reports</span></span></span>

Instead of loud explosive noises, witnesses commonly describe:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* gentle hissing;
* frying or sizzling sounds;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.livescience.com/38651-meteor-sound-electrophonics-perseids.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[livescience.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Can You Hear a Meteor? &#124; Electrophonics &#124; Live Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Can You Hear a Meteor? &#124; Electrophonics &#124; Live Science</span></span></span>
* static-like crackling;
* soft swishing;
* brief popping noises.

</div>

These descriptions resemble the sort of noises expected from small nearby objects vibrating rather than a powerful explosion many kilometres away.

Some surveys have also noted recurring circumstances in [witness accounts]({{ 'witness-accounts/' | relative_url }}). Reports sometimes mention the presence of metal objects nearby, exceptionally dry vegetation, or unusually bright fireballs. A few observers even reported their hair appearing to respond at the same moment as the sound. While such anecdotes are intriguing, they remain observational rather than definitive evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[amsmeteors.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

## Why the evidence remains unsettled

Although electrophonic sounds are taken more seriously today than they once were, they are **not considered a solved phenomenon**.

Several problems continue to challenge researchers.

First, the events are extremely rare. Even among brilliant fireballs, only a small proportion generate simultaneous [sound reports]({{ 'sound-reports/' | relative_url }}), making planned instrumental observations difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930009995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[NASA Technical Reports Server]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Technical Reports Server Meteor fireball sounds identified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Technical Reports ServerMeteor fireball sounds identified - NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS)...</span></span></span>

Second, no single physical model explains every reported case. Different researchers have suggested several mechanisms, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* VLF electromagnetic radiation produced by plasma turbulence;
* electric fields generated during catastrophic fragmentation;
* local corona discharge around nearby objects;
* other forms of atmospheric electrical coupling.

</div>

## Why instant meteor sounds can resemble UFO reports

Within reports of [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) aerial noises, electrophonic meteors occupy a unique niche.

Unlike delayed sonic booms—which can usually be matched with the meteor's trajectory and timing—an instantaneous hiss may leave witnesses convinced that the sound came directly from an object flying overhead. Since the sound coincides perfectly with the visible streak, it can create the impression that the object itself was "making noise" despite its immense altitude.

This timing has occasionally contributed to UFO interpretations, particularly when observers were unfamiliar with electrophonic reports or expected all meteor sounds to be delayed. In reality, if electrophonic sounds occur, the audible source is thought to be the observer's immediate surroundings rather than the meteor itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[amsmeteors.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>
