## Introduction

When people report hearing a strange hum, buzz or other unusual sound at the same time as seeing an unidentified light or object, it is tempting to assume the two experiences came from the same source. Research on perception and eyewitness memory suggests a more cautious interpretation. Under uncertain conditions—particularly at night, during unexpected events or after exposure to UFO stories—people can sincerely connect sights and sounds that were unrelated, misremember when they occurred, or gradually reshape their recollection as they discuss the event or encounter new information. This does not imply dishonesty. Instead, it reflects well-established features of human perception and memory that become especially important when interpreting reports of unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2522502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories - PubMed...</span></span></span>

## Why unfamiliar sounds become linked to unusual lights

A key feature of perception is that the brain tries to combine information from different senses into a single, coherent account of what happened. If a bright light appears overhead and an unusual sound is heard at roughly the same time, people naturally infer that both came from the same object, even when the true source of the sound is uncertain.

This process, known as cross-modal binding, is normally helpful because sights and sounds from the same event usually do belong together. However, in ambiguous situations it can produce mistaken associations. Night-time viewing, poor distance cues, echoes, wind, delayed sound propagation and competing background noises all increase uncertainty about where a sound originated or whether it was connected to the observed light. Experimental neuroscience shows that the brain actively integrates auditory and visual signals into unified percepts, even when the relationship between them is not perfectly clear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nn993" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Neural correlates of cross-modal binding &#124; Nature Neuroscience</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neural correlates of cross-modal binding &#124; Nature Neuroscience...</span></span></span>

In UFO [sound reports]({{ 'sound-reports/' | relative_url }}), this means that a witness may accurately remember seeing an unusual light and accurately remember hearing an unfamiliar noise, yet still be mistaken in concluding that the sound came from the object itself.

### Linking simultaneous sights and sounds

Several factors make these links especially compelling:

* [Low-frequency]({{ 'low-frequency/' | relative_url }}) sounds are difficult for humans to locate accurately.
* Darkness reduces reliable visual reference points for estimating distance and size.
* Unexpected events encourage rapid interpretation before complete information is available.
* Aircraft, distant industry, wildlife or [atmospheric sounds]({{ 'sound-travel/' | relative_url }}) may overlap with an unrelated visual event.

Because the brain prefers one coherent explanation over multiple unrelated ones, witnesses often recall the experience as a single integrated event rather than separate sensory observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nn993" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Neural correlates of cross-modal binding &#124; Nature Neuroscience</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neural correlates of cross-modal binding &#124; Nature Neuroscience...</span></span></span>

## How expectation influences interpretation

Expectation affects perception long before anyone gives a formal witness statement. When people already have a mental model of what a UFO encounter "should" be like, ambiguous sounds are more likely to be interpreted within that framework.

Psychologists refer to this as top-down processing: existing knowledge, beliefs and expectations help interpret incomplete sensory information. If someone has recently watched documentaries, read news coverage about [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) aerial phenomena or discussed UFO cases, those ideas become more readily available when an unusual experience occurs.

This does not mean media exposure creates sightings. Rather, it influences which interpretation feels most plausible when the available sensory information is incomplete. Statistical analysis of large UFO reporting databases has found that reporting activity itself is sensitive to media attention, suggesting that publicity affects both the likelihood of reporting and the way experiences are interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378437122005295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect On the dynamics of reporting data: A case study of UFO sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">On the dynamics of reporting data: A case study of UFO sightings - ScienceDirectOctober 1, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2022</span></span></span>

### Expectation after media coverage

Following periods of intense publicity, witnesses may be more inclined to:

* Interpret an unidentified light and unrelated sound as belonging together.
* Describe sounds using familiar UFO-related language such as "electrical hum", "buzzing" or "vibration".
* Recall additional details after repeated discussion or media exposure.

## How memory changes uncertain events

Memory is reconstructive rather than a perfect recording. Each recollection involves rebuilding the event from stored information, current knowledge and later experiences. As a result, memories can become more detailed, more coherent or more confident over time without necessarily becoming more accurate.

Research led by Elizabeth Loftus and colleagues has repeatedly shown that post-event information can alter what people later remember. Misleading questions, repeated discussions or exposure to alternative accounts may introduce details that become incorporated into an individual's recollection while still feeling entirely genuine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2522502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories - PubMed...</span></span></span>

For UFO sound reports, this can mean that an initially vague memory such as "I heard something unusual" gradually becomes "the object emitted a deep electrical humming noise" after repeated retelling, especially if similar descriptions are encountered elsewhere.

### Why uncertain events are especially vulnerable

Several circumstances increase the likelihood of memory reconstruction:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* Brief observations.
* High emotional arousal or surprise.
* Darkness or poor visibility.
* Divided attention between multiple unusual events.
* Delays before writing or recording an account.
* Repeated conversations with other witnesses.

</div>

## Why sincere witnesses can disagree

One common misconception is that differing accounts automatically indicate fabrication. Cognitive psychology suggests the opposite: honest observers frequently remember different aspects of the same event because attention, viewpoint, stress and later reconstruction vary between individuals.

One witness may primarily remember the unusual sound, another the movement of the light and a third the emotional impact of the experience. As memories evolve independently, later comparisons can reveal genuine disagreements over timing, sequence or the characteristics of the reported noise.

Modern memory research further indicates that expectations at the time of recall—not only at the time of observation—can influence remembered event details, reinforcing the importance of documenting [witness accounts]({{ 'witness-accounts/' | relative_url }}) as soon as possible after an incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.3003886" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.plos.org">[PLOS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.plos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shared memories of event details in the human brain are altered by misinformation and test expectations &#124; PLOS BiologyJuly 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 6, 2026</span></span></span>

## What this means when evaluating UFO sound reports

Expectation and memory research neither proves nor disproves any individual UFO report. Instead, it provides a framework for understanding why unusual sound descriptions should be interpreted carefully, particularly when they depend on retrospective recollection under uncertain conditions.

A balanced assessment distinguishes between several separate questions:

* **Was an unusual sound genuinely heard?** Quite possibly.
* **Was the sound accurately described?** It may have been, but later reconstruction can alter details.
* **Did the sound originate from the reported object?** That cannot be assumed solely from simultaneous perception.
* **Does a confident memory guarantee accuracy?** No. Confidence and accuracy are related only imperfectly, especially after repeated recall or exposure to post-event information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2522502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories - PubMed...</span></span></span>

Understanding these psychological mechanisms helps explain why UFO sound reports often contain vivid, sincerely remembered experiences while still leaving open the possibility that perception and memory combined separate or ambiguous events into a single narrative.
