## Introduction

A bright fireball can vanish in seconds, yet the loud boom associated with it may not arrive until several minutes later. That delay is one of the main reasons meteor-generated sonic booms are sometimes mistaken for [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) aerial sounds or even UFO-related events. The apparent separation between the flash and the explosion is not mysterious: it is a direct consequence of light travelling vastly faster than sound, combined with the great height at which most fireballs fragment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

For many witnesses, the visual event has already faded from memory by the time windows rattle or a deep explosion is heard. ## How light and sound travel at different speeds

The key mechanism is the enormous difference between the speed of light and the speed of sound.

The light from a meteor reaches an observer essentially instantaneously over atmospheric distances. Even if a fireball fragments 50 kilometres above the ground, the light takes only a tiny fraction of a second to arrive. Human [perception]({{ 'perception/' | relative_url }}) therefore treats the flash as immediate.

The pressure wave generated by the same event behaves very differently. Once created, the shock eventually weakens into an audible sonic boom that travels through the atmosphere at roughly the speed of sound—about 343 metres per second near sea level under typical conditions, although temperature, pressure and wind alter the exact value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

Because sound is comparatively slow, even a simple estimate shows why delays are expected:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A boom from around 30 km away can take roughly 90 seconds to arrive.
* A source about 50 km away can produce a delay approaching two and a half minutes.
* More distant or higher-altitude airbursts can produce waits of three, four or more minutes before the sound reaches observers.

</div>

The delay is therefore a normal feature of the physics rather than evidence that two separate events occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

## How altitude and distance shape the delay

The waiting time depends on more than the meteor's altitude alone. What matters is the distance the sound must travel through the atmosphere from the point where the strongest shock wave is generated to the listener.

A large fireball may fragment between roughly 20 and 50 kilometres above Earth's surface. Someone directly beneath the event experiences a shorter sound path than someone observing it near the horizon, where the boom must travel both downward and sideways through the atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

Several additional factors influence when—and even whether—the boom is heard:

* **Fragmentation height:** A lower-altitude breakup generally produces a shorter delay and a stronger boom.
* **Observer location:** People tens of kilometres apart can hear the same event at noticeably different times.
* **Atmospheric conditions:** Wind patterns and temperature layers can bend, weaken or focus sound waves, making booms audible much farther away than expected in some situations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/sonic-booms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Geological Survey Sonic Booms &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological Survey Sonic Booms &#124; U.S. Geological Survey</span></span></span>

These effects explain why reports from different communities often describe the same meteor but disagree about exactly when the explosion was heard.

## Why witnesses often miss the connection

The delayed arrival of sound creates a psychological disconnect between what people see and what they later hear.

If someone watches a brilliant meteor streak overhead, they may naturally assume the event is over once the light disappears. By the time an explosive bang arrives several minutes later, many have resumed whatever they were doing and no longer associate the sound with the earlier flash.

Others never make the connection because they experience only one part of the event:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* People indoors may hear the boom but never see the meteor.
* Drivers may notice the explosion after the fireball has passed out of view.
* Daytime observers may hear windows shake without ever noticing the brief flash against a bright sky.
* Residents facing away from the meteor's path may hear only the delayed pressure wave.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/faqf/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">American Meteor Society Fireball FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyFireball FAQs - American Meteor Society...</span></span></span>

</div>

This separation in perception makes the boom seem spontaneous, encouraging reports of unexplained explosions, military activity or unidentified flying objects until meteor observations and camera footage reconstruct the event.

## A real-world pattern seen repeatedly

Documented meteor events repeatedly follow this sequence: a brilliant fireball appears, several minutes pass, and only then do widespread reports of booming sounds and shaking buildings emerge.

For example, a large meteor over the north-eastern United States in 2026 produced a daylight fireball followed by delayed sonic booms that were initially mistaken by many residents for an earthquake or explosion. This pattern is characteristic of large fireballs. The visual event and the audible boom originate from the same phenomenon, but because light arrives almost instantly while sound takes minutes to cross the atmosphere, the two experiences become separated in time. That delay is one of the simplest—and most important—reasons [meteor booms]({{ 'meteor-booms/' | relative_url }}) are so often reported as mysterious or UFO-related noises before their true cause is established.
