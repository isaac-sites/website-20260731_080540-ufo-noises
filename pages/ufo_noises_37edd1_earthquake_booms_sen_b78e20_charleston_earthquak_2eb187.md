## Introduction

The 1886 Charleston earthquake remains one of the strongest historical examples of how earthquakes can generate loud, mysterious sounds that are easily mistaken for explosions or other [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) phenomena. Within the broader subject of earthquake booms and Seneca Guns, Charleston is especially important because contemporary witnesses documented not only the roaring noise of the main earthquake but also weeks of aftershocks accompanied by repeated cannon-like detonations. These accounts provide one of the clearest historical records linking audible booms directly to seismic activity rather than to atmospheric or unidentified aerial events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquake-booms-seneca-guns-and-other-sounds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>

Unlike isolated reports of unexplained bangs, the Charleston sequence combined thousands of eyewitness observations with an extended series of aftershocks. That unusually rich historical record allows modern seismologists to compare reported sounds with known earthquake behaviour and better understand why some [shallow earthquakes]({{ 'shallow-quakes/' | relative_url }}) produce booming noises while others do not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/1886-charleston-south-carolina-earthquake-intensities-and-ground-motions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1886 charleston south carolina earthquake intensities and ground motions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyThe 1886 Charleston, South Carolina, earthquake: Intensities and ground motions &#124; U.S. Geological Survey...</span></span></span>

The roaring became part of the event's defining memory because it spread across an exceptionally large area. The earthquake itself, now generally estimated at about magnitude 7.3, was felt across much of the eastern United States, and reports of the accompanying roar appeared throughout the region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubs.usgs.gov/circ/1986/0985/report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubs.usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubs.usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charleston, South Carolina, Earthquake-March 28, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 28, 2012</span></span></span>

Modern seismology explains these descriptions without requiring any unusual acoustic source. As seismic waves travelled through the shallow crust, high-frequency vibrations reached the ground surface and produced audible sound. Near the epicentral region, the sound and shaking were experienced almost simultaneously, making the earth itself appear to roar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquake-booms-seneca-guns-and-other-sounds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>

## Why the aftershocks produced repeated detonations

The main earthquake was only the beginning. For weeks afterwards, Charleston experienced frequent aftershocks, many of which were accompanied by loud explosive noises. The US Geological Survey specifically notes historical reports describing "loud detonations" during this prolonged aftershock sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquake-booms-seneca-guns-and-other-sounds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>

Several factors made these aftershocks particularly noticeable.

First, the fault system remained highly active. Large earthquakes redistribute stress through surrounding rocks, allowing numerous smaller ruptures to occur as the crust gradually adjusted. Some of these aftershocks were strong enough to damage already weakened buildings. Modern reassessments of historical records have even identified a previously unrecognised sizeable aftershock roughly nine to ten minutes after the main shock, estimated at about magnitude 5.6.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/1886-charleston-south-carolina-earthquake-intensities-and-ground-motions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1886 charleston south carolina earthquake intensities and ground motions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyThe 1886 Charleston, South Carolina, earthquake: Intensities and ground motions &#124; U.S. Geological Survey...</span></span></span>

Second, many aftershocks were probably relatively shallow. Shallow earthquakes transmit higher-frequency vibrations to the surface more efficiently than deeper events, making audible booming much more likely. This is the same mechanism now recognised in many modern reports of earthquake sounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquake-booms-seneca-guns-and-other-sounds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>

Third, people were already on alert. Following the destruction of the main earthquake, residents remained outside or slept in temporary shelters because they feared additional shocks. This meant thousands of observers were awake and listening during every significant aftershock, greatly increasing the number of documented [sound reports]({{ 'sound-reports/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubs.usgs.gov/circ/1986/0985/report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubs.usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubs.usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Charleston, South Carolina, Earthquake-March 28, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 28, 2012</span></span></span>

## Why Charleston became such an important historical case

Many mysterious boom reports suffer from one major problem: there is no confirmed source. Charleston differs because the source was unmistakable.

Researchers can directly compare:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the timing of documented aftershocks;
* reports of shaking;
* descriptions of booming or roaring;
* patterns of structural damage.

</div>

Because these independent observations align so well, Charleston serves as a benchmark demonstrating that earthquakes can genuinely produce loud, explosion-like sounds rather than merely being associated with them through coincidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/1886-charleston-south-carolina-earthquake-intensities-and-ground-motions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1886 charleston south carolina earthquake intensities and ground motions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyThe 1886 Charleston, South Carolina, earthquake: Intensities and ground motions &#124; U.S. Geological Survey...</span></span></span>

The event also illustrates that audible earthquake sounds are not restricted to the initial rupture. A prolonged aftershock sequence can generate repeated booms over days or weeks, reinforcing local traditions that an area is haunted by mysterious cannon-like noises.

## How Charleston helps explain later mystery booms

The Charleston sequence provides a useful comparison for later reports of unexplained booms along the Atlantic coast.

Without historical context, a resident hearing a sudden cannon-like bang with little obvious shaking might reasonably suspect:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* an explosion;
* military activity;
* a sonic boom;
* an unidentified object overhead.

</div>

Charleston demonstrates that another explanation must also be considered: a small, shallow earthquake whose acoustic energy is more noticeable than its ground motion. The USGS specifically discusses the Charleston earthquake alongside Seneca Guns because both involve reports of loud detonations that can originate from natural geological processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquake-booms-seneca-guns-and-other-sounds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>

Seneca Guns almost certainly encompass several different natural and human-made causes depending on location and circumstance. ## What the Charleston record teaches modern investigators

The Charleston earthquake highlights several practical lessons that remain relevant when evaluating reports of unexplained booming sounds:

* **Booms may accompany only modest shaking.** Especially during shallow events, witnesses may remember the sound more vividly than the ground motion itself.
* **Repeated detonations do not necessarily indicate repeated explosions.** They may instead reflect an ongoing sequence of aftershocks as stressed faults continue to adjust.
* **Historical eyewitness accounts have scientific value.** Thousands of descriptions collected after the Charleston earthquake have helped researchers reconstruct both shaking intensity and the behaviour of the aftershock sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/publications/1886-charleston-south-carolina-earthquake-intensities-and-ground-motions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1886 charleston south carolina earthquake intensities and ground motions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Geological SurveyThe 1886 Charleston, South Carolina, earthquake: Intensities and ground motions &#124; U.S. Geological Survey...</span></span></span>
* **Context matters.** A confirmed earthquake sequence provides a much stronger explanation for mysterious booms than speculation about aerial phenomena when the timing and location are consistent with seismic activity.

Within the wider study of [UFO noises]({{ 'ufo-noises/' | relative_url }}) and other unexplained acoustic events, Charleston remains one of the strongest historical demonstrations that the Earth itself can generate sounds dramatic enough to resemble artillery, thunder or distant explosions. Rather than being an obscure curiosity, its documented roaring main shock and booming aftershocks provide a well-supported natural analogue against which later mystery-boom reports can be evaluated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquake-booms-seneca-guns-and-other-sounds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usgs.gov">[U.S. Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usgs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on usgs.gov.</span></span></span>
