## Introduction

Media coverage of unidentified flying objects (UFOs) and unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) can influence how people interpret ambiguous sounds without creating the underlying event itself. When someone hears a distant hum, buzz or vibration while observing an unusual light, prior exposure to documentaries, news reports, films or online discussions can make a UFO explanation feel more immediately plausible than competing explanations. This effect reflects well-established psychological processes involving expectation, top-down [perception]({{ 'perception/' | relative_url }}) and reconstructive memory rather than evidence that media generate sightings. Research on perception and eyewitness memory suggests that expectations are particularly influential when sensory information is incomplete or uncertain, making this an important consideration when evaluating reports of alleged [UFO noises]({{ 'ufo-noises/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25538601/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Top-down influences on ambiguous perception: the role of stable and transient states of the observer - PubMedDecember 8, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 8, 2014</span></span></span>

## How expectations guide ambiguous perception

Perception is not a passive recording of the outside world. The brain continually combines incoming sensory information with previous knowledge, beliefs and recent experiences to arrive at the most likely interpretation of an event. This process, known as top-down processing, is especially important when sights or sounds are faint, distant or difficult to identify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25538601/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Top-down influences on ambiguous perception: the role of stable and transient states of the observer - PubMedDecember 8, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 8, 2014</span></span></span>

In the context of UFO reports, media exposure supplies ready-made expectations about what an encounter should sound like. Popular documentaries and dramatised reconstructions often describe mysterious craft as producing:

* A [low-frequency]({{ 'low-frequency/' | relative_url }}) hum.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* An electrical buzzing noise.
* A pulsating vibration.
* An unusual silence interrupted by mechanical sounds.

</div>

After repeated exposure, these descriptions become familiar reference points. If a person later encounters an [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) environmental sound while observing an unusual light, those familiar patterns may become the first explanation that comes to mind. This does not mean the witness invents the sound. Rather, expectation influences how an already ambiguous experience is interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25538601/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Top-down influences on ambiguous perception: the role of stable and transient states of the observer - PubMedDecember 8, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 8, 2014</span></span></span>

Importantly, the same ambiguous sound could have been interpreted differently in another cultural or historical setting. Before the modern UFO era, unfamiliar aerial sounds were more likely to be attributed to atmospheric phenomena, secret aircraft or other locally familiar explanations.

## Why publicity can increase reporting activity

Periods of intense publicity surrounding UFO stories have long been associated with increases in reported sightings. Researchers studying UFO reporting databases have noted that reporting rates fluctuate alongside major media attention, suggesting that publicity affects whether people decide that an unusual experience is worth reporting and how they categorise it. This pattern is consistent with broader psychological research on attention and expectation rather than serving as evidence that the underlying phenomenon itself has become more common.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25538601/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Top-down influences on ambiguous perception: the role of stable and transient states of the observer - PubMedDecember 8, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 8, 2014</span></span></span>

Several mechanisms help explain this relationship.

**Greater vigilance.** People who have recently watched a high-profile documentary or followed extensive news coverage may become more attentive to unusual lights and unfamiliar environmental sounds that they would normally ignore.

**Lower reporting threshold.** An experience that previously might have been dismissed as an odd [aircraft noise]({{ 'aircraft-noise/' | relative_url }}) may instead be regarded as potentially significant because similar descriptions have recently appeared in the media.

**Shared interpretive framework.** Publicity provides common language and expectations that make people more confident that their experience resembles those described by other witnesses.

This helps explain why clusters of reports sometimes follow widely publicised cases even when there is no independent evidence that unusual aerial activity increased during the same period.

## How familiar UFO language enters later accounts

Witness statements often evolve over time rather than remaining fixed. Psychological research on eyewitness memory shows that memories are reconstructed whenever they are recalled, making them vulnerable to new information introduced after the original event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://staff.washington.edu/eloftus/Articles/hoff.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: staff.washington.edu">[UW Staff]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">staff.washington.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UW StaffMisinformation and Memory The Creation of New Memories (1989)March 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 1989</span></span></span>

For UFO reports, this means that descriptions of sound may gradually become more specific after:

* Watching television programmes about famous UFO encounters.
* Reading articles comparing multiple [witness accounts]({{ 'witness-accounts/' | relative_url }}).
* Participating in online discussion forums.
* Talking repeatedly with other witnesses or investigators.

Instead of recalling an uncertain "strange noise", a witness may later remember an "electrical hum", "vibrating resonance" or "metallic buzzing" because those labels have become familiar within UFO culture. The change may occur sincerely rather than deliberately, reflecting the normal reconstructive nature of memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://staff.washington.edu/eloftus/Articles/hoff.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: staff.washington.edu">[washington.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">staff.washington.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UW StaffMisinformation and Memory The Creation of New Memories (1989)March 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 1989</span></span></span>

Research on eyewitness testimony also shows that ambiguous events are especially susceptible to schema-driven recall. A schema is a mental framework for understanding familiar situations. When information is incomplete, people naturally fill gaps using the schema that seems to fit best. In experimental settings, participants exposed to ambiguous events are more likely to recall details consistent with an activated schema, even when those details were absent from the original experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12877270/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The influence of schemas, stimulus ambiguity, and interview schedule on eyewitness memory over time - PubMed...</span></span></span>

## Historical changes in what witnesses report hearing

Descriptions of UFO sounds have not remained constant across decades. Historical collections of reports show that [witness language]({{ 'witness-language/' | relative_url }}) often reflects the technology and cultural imagery of the period.

During the 1950s and 1960s, reports frequently referred to humming discs, jet-like roaring or mechanical whirring, echoing contemporary aviation and industrial technology. Later decades saw more references to electronic buzzing, electromagnetic effects and sophisticated propulsion concepts as these ideas became common in science fiction, documentaries and public discussion.

The broad pattern does not necessarily indicate that witnesses were describing different external phenomena. Instead, it illustrates how available cultural vocabulary provides convenient ways of describing unusual experiences whose acoustic characteristics may have been difficult to identify precisely.

## Media influence has limits

The influence of media should not be overstated. Exposure to UFO stories does not automatically produce sightings, nor does it explain every report involving unusual sounds. Many witnesses describe experiences before learning of similar accounts, while others report sounds that bear little resemblance to common UFO narratives.

Psychological evidence instead supports a more limited conclusion: media exposure can alter which interpretation feels most convincing when sensory evidence is uncertain. It may also influence the language used during later recollection, particularly after repeated discussion or prolonged reflection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25538601/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Top-down influences on ambiguous perception: the role of stable and transient states of the observer - PubMedDecember 8, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 8, 2014</span></span></span>

This distinction is important because it separates two different questions:

* **Did the witness genuinely experience an unusual sound?** Quite possibly.
* **Was the sound necessarily produced by the observed object, or remembered exactly as first perceived?** That remains much less certain when expectation and later information could have shaped interpretation.

## What this means for interpreting UFO sound reports

Understanding media effects does not require dismissing witness testimony. Instead, it encourages separating the original sensory experience from later interpretation. Investigators often gain the clearest information by documenting exactly what a witness heard before discussing famous UFO cases or introducing comparisons with other reports.

Viewed in this way, media influence becomes one factor among several that shape UFO [sound reports]({{ 'sound-reports/' | relative_url }}). It neither proves nor disproves extraordinary claims. Rather, it helps explain why descriptions of ambiguous noises often become more consistent with familiar UFO narratives after periods of publicity, repeated storytelling or exposure to widely recognised encounter accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://staff.washington.edu/eloftus/Articles/hoff.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: staff.washington.edu">[washington.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">staff.washington.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UW StaffMisinformation and Memory The Creation of New Memories (1989)March 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 1989</span></span></span>
